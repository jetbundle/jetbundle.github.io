chapter 1 — basic mechanics

from galilean inertia to cartan fields of extremals

Classical mechanics begins with a small set of experimentally verified principles and is progressively abstracted into a coordinate-free geometric theory on manifolds. The passage from Newton’s second law to Lagrangian and variational formalisms is not a fashion: it is the discovery that equations of motion ought to remain valid under arbitrary changes of coordinates, and that symmetries and conservation laws are two sides of the same geometric coin.

newtonian foundations

Empirical observation is blunt but durable. In an inertial frame a free particle moves uniformly and rectilinearly. When a force is applied, the acceleration scales with that force. Space-time is modelled as an affine four-dimensional manifold equipped with a Galilean structure: a preferred absolute time function $t$ whose level sets are Euclidean three-spaces, together with a Euclidean metric on each spatial slice. The Galilean group — spatial rotations, translations, and uniform boosts — is the group of symmetries of that structure.

Definition. Newton’s principle of determinacy asserts that the state of a mechanical system at any instant is completely fixed by the positions and velocities of all particles at that instant. Consequently the equations of motion are second-order ordinary differential equations on configuration space.

For a system of $N$ particles the configuration space is $\mathbb{R}^{3N}$ (or a submanifold thereof when constraints are present), and the Newtonian equations read

$$ m_i\ddot{\mathbf{q}}_i=\mathbf{F}_i(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}},t),\qquad i=1,\dots,N. $$

Remark. The mathematical motivation for the affine-Galilean model is that it is the unique structure (up to isomorphism) compatible with the observed invariance under the Galilean group and with the absolute character of time intervals. Absolute time is already a geometric statement: “duration” is not redefined by how you move through the slices.

Exercise 1.1. In inertial coordinates a free particle satisfies $\ddot{\mathbf{q}}=0$. Show that under a Galilean boost $\mathbf{q}'=\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{v}t$ with constant $\mathbf{v}$ the motion remains free, and compute the boosted velocity and acceleration.

systems of low dimension and central forces

For a single particle in a conservative force field $\mathbf{F}=-\nabla V$ the Newtonian equation reduces to

$$ m\ddot{\mathbf{q}}=-\nabla V(\mathbf{q}). $$

When the force is central, $V=V(r)$ with $r=|\mathbf{q}|$, angular momentum $\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{q}\times m\dot{\mathbf{q}}$ is conserved. Conservation of $\mathbf{L}$ implies that the motion is planar and that the radial motion is governed by an effective one-dimensional potential

$$ V_{\mathrm{eff}}(r)=V(r)+\frac{\ell^2}{2mr^2}, $$

where $\ell=|\mathbf{L}|$. The phase portrait of the radial equation is therefore completely determined by the critical points and level sets of $V_{\mathrm{eff}}$ — geometry of a single function on the half-line $r>0$.

The Kepler problem $V(r)=-k/r$ admits the explicit solution

$$ r=\frac{p}{1+e\cos\theta}, $$

where $p=\ell^2/(mk)$ is the semi-latus rectum and $e$ is the eccentricity. The orbit is a conic section with one focus at the origin.

Theorem (Bertrand). Up to scaling, the only central force laws that produce closed bounded orbits for all initial data that are energetically allowed are the harmonic oscillator and the inverse-square law.

Remark. The geometric origin of Kepler’s conics is the isotropy of Euclidean space: rotational invariance plus the inverse-square law yield the famous focal property of the ellipse. Geometry is not decoration on top of the force law; it selects which force laws look “special” among many conceivable radial potentials.

Exercise 1.2. For the isotropic harmonic oscillator $V(r)=\tfrac12 kr^2$, construct $V_{\mathrm{eff}}$, find circular orbits of angular momentum $\ell$, and show that small radial oscillations have frequency equal to the angular frequency of the circular orbit (closed Lissajous curves).

Exercise 1.3 (Kepler). Starting from energy $E$ and angular momentum $\ell$, reduce the radial motion to a quadrature $t=\int dr/\sqrt{(2/m)(E-V_{\mathrm{eff}}(r))}$ for $V=-k/r$, and recover the bound/unbound dichotomy for $E<0$ and $E\geq 0$.

lagrangian formalism

Very quickly the coordinate expression $m\ddot q=F$ becomes inefficient: change coordinates and the second derivatives rearrange themselves messily. The better object is an action.

Definition (Hamilton’s principle). The actual trajectory $q(t)$ between fixed end-points realises a critical point of the action functional

$$ S[q]=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}L(q,\dot{q},t)\,dt. $$

The first variation $\delta S=0$ yields the Euler–Lagrange equations

$$ \frac{d}{dt}\Bigl(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}^i}\Bigr)-\frac{\partial L}{\partial q^i}=0. $$

These equations are covariant under arbitrary (sufficiently smooth) changes of coordinates on configuration space. The variational principle itself is intrinsically geometric: it depends only on the values of the Lagrangian along curves and not on any particular coordinate chart.

Configuration space $Q$ is taken to be a smooth manifold. The Lagrangian becomes a smooth function $L:TQ\to\mathbb{R}$ on the tangent bundle. The Euler–Lagrange equations are then intrinsically defined as the critical-point equations of the action on the space of paths in $Q$. Motion is no longer a curve in Euclidean space by default; it is an integral curve of a second-order dynamics on $TQ$.

Exercise 1.4. Take $L=\tfrac12 m(\dot x^2+\dot y^2)-V(\sqrt{x^2+y^2})$ in Cartesian coordinates, pass to polar coordinates $(r,\theta)$, and write the Euler–Lagrange equations for $r$ and $\theta$. Recover conservation of $\ell=mr^2\dot\theta$ without referring to $\mathbf{q}\times m\dot{\mathbf{q}}$ by name.

noether’s theorem

If a one-parameter group of diffeomorphisms $\Phi_s:Q\to Q$ leaves the Lagrangian invariant,

$$ L(T\Phi_s(v))=L(v)\qquad\text{for all }v\in TQ, $$

then the quantity

$$ I(v)=\Bigl\langle\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}},\xi_Q(q)\Bigr\rangle $$

is constant along solutions, where $\xi_Q$ is the infinitesimal generator of $\Phi_s$.

Theorem (Noether). Continuous symmetries of the Lagrangian produce first integrals. This is the geometric origin of the classical conservation laws of energy, momentum, and angular momentum — energy arising when the formalism is completed carefully with explicit time dependence, momentum with translational invariance, angular momentum with rotations.

Proof (sketch). Differentiate the invariance identity in $s$ at $s=0$, rewrite the resulting directional derivative of $L$ along solutions using the Euler–Lagrange equation, and observe that $dI/dt=0$ along the dynamical vector field on $TQ$. The pairing with $\xi_Q$ is coordinate-free once $\partial L/\partial\dot q$ is recognized as a fiber derivative (momentum map in embryonic form).

Exercise 1.5. For free particle $L=\tfrac12 m|\dot q|^2$ on $\mathbb{R}^3$, take the one-parameter group of translations along a fixed unit vector $e$. Compute $\xi_Q$, the Noether integral $I$, and identify it with linear momentum along $e$.

small oscillations and parametric resonance

Near a stable equilibrium $q_0$ one expands the Lagrangian to quadratic order. The resulting linear system is

$$ M\ddot{\xi}+K\xi=0, $$

where $M$ and $K$ are the mass and stiffness matrices. The characteristic frequencies are the square roots of the eigenvalues of the generalised eigenproblem $K\mathbf{v}=\omega^2 M\mathbf{v}$.

Remark. When the stiffness matrix depends periodically on time (parametric excitation), Mathieu or Hill equations appear and instability bands arise even while instantaneous frequencies remain real — this is parametric resonance. Geometry of the linearised problem no longer reduces to “eigenvalues of a fixed matrix”: Floquet multipliers enter, and the modern reading of instability is about monodromy of a linear system over a period circle.

Exercise 1.6. For a linear system $M\ddot\xi+K\xi=0$ with $M$ and $K$ symmetric, $M$ positive definite, prove that the eigenvalues of $M^{-1}K$ are real and that the modes can be chosen $M$-orthogonal. What happens if $K$ is indefinite?

rigid-body dynamics

The configuration space of a free rigid body is the rotation group $SO(3)$. In a body-fixed frame the kinetic energy is a quadratic form on the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{so}(3)\simeq\mathbb{R}^3$, and the Euler–Poincaré equations reduce to Euler’s rigid-body equations

$$ \mathbf{I}\dot{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}+\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\mathbf{I}\boldsymbol{\Omega}=0. $$

Poinsot’s geometric construction realises the motion as the rolling without slipping of the inertia ellipsoid on a fixed plane; the polhode and herpolhode curves encode the body and space trajectories of the angular-velocity vector. Coriolis forces appear naturally when equations are written in a rotating frame — geometry of relative motion, not an extra “fictitious” ontology glued onto Newton.

Exercise 1.7. For an axisymmetric rigid body $I_1=I_2\neq I_3$, show that $\Omega_3$ is constant and that $(\Omega_1,\Omega_2)$ precesses uniformly in body frame. Identify the conserved kinetic energy and squared angular momentum on the $(\Omega_1,\Omega_2,\Omega_3)$ sphere.

dissipative extensions

Friction linear in velocity is incorporated by Rayleigh’s dissipation function $R(\dot q)$. The modified Euler–Lagrange equations become

$$ \frac{d}{dt}\Bigl(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}}\Bigr)-\frac{\partial L}{\partial q}=-\frac{\partial R}{\partial\dot{q}}. $$

Remark. On a Riemannian manifold the resulting dynamics is a gradient flow of the energy with respect to the metric defined by the kinetic energy and the Rayleigh form. The same structure underlies the abstract theory of electric circuits, in which voltages and currents are conjugate variables and resistive elements generate a dissipation metric. Mechanics is not “only frictionless orbits”; dissipation has geometry too.

Exercise 1.8. Take $L=\tfrac12 m\dot x^2-\tfrac12 kx^2$ and $R=\tfrac12 c\dot x^2$ with $c>0$. Derive the equation of motion and show that total mechanical energy $E=\tfrac12 m\dot x^2+\tfrac12 kx^2$ satisfies $\dot E=-c\dot x^2\leq 0$.

variational calculus and optimal control

Carathéodory’s approach constructs a field of extremals whose associated Cartan form

$$ \theta_L=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}^i}\,dq^i+\Bigl(L-\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}^i}\dot{q}^i\Bigr)dt $$

is closed. The Hamilton–Jacobi equation is the condition that the Lagrangian coincides with the total differential of a generating function. The same geometric construction, when the admissible velocities are restricted to a control set, yields Pontryagin’s maximum principle: an optimal trajectory maximises the Pontryagin Hamiltonian over the control variables.

Remark. This is the first clear preview of control theory as a chapter of the mechanical program rather than a foreign engineering dialect. Optimality inherits the extremal field; controls simply restrict the admissible “velocities” of the geometric picture.

Exercise 1.9. For the free particle $L=\tfrac12 m\dot q^2$ on $\mathbb{R}$, write the Cartan form $\theta_L$ in coordinates $(q,v,t)$ with $v=\dot q$, and verify $d\theta_L=0$ along solutions of the Euler–Lagrange equation (or along the full jet if you prefer the full exterior calculus statement).

comparison of formalisms

feature lagrangian hamiltonian optimal control
base space tangent bundle $TQ$ cotangent bundle $T^*Q$ state–input space $X\times U$
primary function Lagrangian $L(q,\dot q,t)$ Hamiltonian $H(q,p,t)$ cost / Pontryagin Hamiltonian
governing principle critical action symplectic volume, Ham. flow Pontryagin maximum principle
symmetry consequence Noether first integrals momentum map $\mu$ controllability / Chow–Rashevsky

The Lagrangian picture is natural for unconstrained variational problems and for systems with configuration-space symmetries. The Hamiltonian picture isolates the symplectic geometry of phase space and is indispensable for perturbation theory and integrability — that move will return in the symplectic chapter. Optimal-control theory enlarges the variational setting to include non-holonomic velocity constraints and bounded controls, while retaining the same skeleton of Cartan forms and extremal fields.

Taken together, these constructions convert the empirical Newtonian theory into a coherent differential-geometric framework in which the equations of motion, the conservation laws, and the variational principles are manifestations of the same underlying manifold structure. That is the basic mechanics we need before ordinary differential equations, Lie theory, and dynamical systems become fully legible as mechanical geometry rather than as catalogs of techniques.
