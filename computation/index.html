computation

there is really no single right way to self-directed andragogy. i say that plainly because this section should be read with humility: it is supplemental notes, not a curriculum, not a certificate, and not a claim to completeness in any direction.

andragogy is nevertheless required for scientists and engineers who perform research at a high level. you cannot outsource every mental model to a course schedule. at some point you must assemble, test, and revise your own picture of how machines, information, and numerical evolution fit together. this is my attempt at that assembly for myself, offered in case it helps you assemble yours.

the working draft lives in basics of computation. i formalize computation the way a physicist might formalize mechanics: begin with the machine as a finite physical system, treat information as distinguishability of states, place memory before syntax, and only then introduce languages as representations rather than foundations. the treatise walks through the information that is physically represented (addresses, bytes, cache lines, timesteps) alongside the mathematical laws pertinent to the machine (state evolution, entropy, operators, stability, memory geometry). each section pairs prose with small interactive demonstrations on the page itself.

this will change over time. gaps will remain. if you treat it as a complete account of computer science or scientific computing, you will be disappointed. if you treat it as a mental image to stress-test against real code and real hardware, it may earn its keep.
